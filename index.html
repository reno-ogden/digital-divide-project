Connected is not the same as Included.

If your organization funds, builds, or runs digital-access programs, this argument is for you. Treat an internet connection as the starting point, not the finish line. People also need a device that works for the task, practical skills, accessible services, and someone to turn to when they get stuck.

Part 01

Reliable connection

A person needs internet service that is affordable, dependable, and strong enough for essential tasks.

When it is present

Learning, work, healthcare, and communication remain reachable.

When it is missing

People may ration data, lose work, or depend on public Wi-Fi.

Program response Affordable plans, stable infrastructure, and clear enrollment support.

Evidence behind the argument

Access alone misses the barriers that decide whether people can participate.

These sources focus on different groups and measure different parts of the problem. Together, they show why access, skills, design, support, and larger institutional choices all matter.

Context matters. Pew studied readiness for online learning, while AFB studied accessibility barriers faced by blind and low-vision adults. I am not treating either group as a picture of everyone’s digital experience.

The rest of the picture

Six more sources show what connection numbers leave out.

Taken together, the research follows a clear path: access quality affects what is possible, skills and support shape what people can do, and institutional choices decide whether help lasts.

Access quality

More people are online, but they are not connected in the same way.

NTIA reported 13 million more internet users from 2021 to 2023, while lower-income households and several racial and ethnic groups were still more likely to rely only on smartphones.

Goldberg, 2024 ↗

Connected layers

Digital opportunity depends on more than one program or service.

The Urban Institute connects infrastructure, access, skills, online safety, and effective use. If one layer is weak, the benefits of the others can be limited too.

Junod et al., 2025 ↗

Unequal outcomes

The divide continues after someone gets a device and a connection.

Reisdorf and Rhinesmith separate material access from skills and from the outcomes people receive. This helps explain why similar access can still lead to very different opportunities.

Reisdorf & Rhinesmith, 2020 ↗

Personal support

Handing out a device does not teach someone how to feel comfortable using it.

In a Baltimore program for 20 older adults, participants said the one-on-one training was the most useful part. Support made the technology feel less unfamiliar and stressful.

Gleason & Suen, 2022 ↗

Community programs

Digital navigators help, but the organizations supporting them need resources too.

Research at ten Texas libraries found that programs adjusted to local needs, but staffing, funding, procurement, and uneven partnerships limited what they could keep offering.

Strover et al., 2024 ↗

Structural responsibility

Local support cannot fix every market or policy problem on its own.

Philadelphia’s pandemic response showed how limited data, logistics, funding, provider resistance, and digital redlining can keep unequal access in place.

Popiel & Pickard, 2022 ↗

What program leaders can do

Fund the whole path, not just the first step.

Before starting, renewing, or evaluating a digital-access program, ask whether people can complete the tasks that matter safely, accessibly, and with the level of help they need.

01

Plan

Start by listening to the people the program is meant to serve.

Ask what they actually need to do online, and include disabled users when testing websites, forms, and tools.

02

Deliver

Build the other supports alongside the connection.

Provide devices that fit the task, practical training, accessible services, and patient technical help.

03

Evaluate

Measure whether people can finish, not only what was handed out.

Track whether people can learn, apply, communicate, and solve problems with the support available to them.

Five-question program check

Before you call the program inclusive, can you answer yes?

0 of 5 commitments selected. A connection alone is only the first step.

Do not stop at, “Did we connect someone?” Also ask, “Could they actually finish what they came to do?”
